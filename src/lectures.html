Lecture Notes

What Are Requirements

What leads to a useful product?

  • Situations where the developers have understood:
    • What the product intends to accomplish
    • How it must accomplish this purpose
    • What kind of work the users want to do
    • How the product affects work and fits into the organization’s goals
  • A product’s requirements are:
    • What it does for the users
    • Constraints it must satisfy in a particular context
  • Requirements process:
    • Exploration of intended product
    • Discovering behavior and functionality
    • Describing outcomes of the above activities
  • Requirements are:
    • Independent of development process, programming language, development tools, etc.
    • Expressed as abstractions
    • Technology neutral

Requirements vs. Design

Requirement: Statement of business need without implementation bias

Design is a plan that:

  • Translates requirements into physical reality
  • Determination of:
    • Devices to be used
    • Software components
    • How construction will be done

Requirements, design, and implementation can be done in an iterative loop

Requirements Gathering and Systems Modeling

  • There is a significant overlap
  • User models help find requirements
  • Requirements are used to model functionality and data
  • Both generate artifacts that can be used to understand and specify requirements

Evolution of Requirements

  • Requirements are not invariant and may evolve
  • Users may demand more functionality
  • In an agile process, partial product may have an effect on work practices leading to new requirements
  • Evolution of requirement cannot be controlled, it has to be accepted
  • The requirements process needs to explicitly take this into account

Requirements & Agile Development

We are uncovering better ways of developing software by doing it and helping others do it. Through this work we have come to value:

  • Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
  • Working software over comprehensive documentation
  • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
  • Responding to change over following a plan

That is, while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more.

What is a requirement?

Functional Requirements:

  • Action that a product must take to be useful to the user
  • Arise from work that stakeholders do

Example: The product shall produce an amended de-icing schedule when a change to a truck status means that the previously scheduled work cannot be carried out as planned

Non-Functional Requirements:

  • Critical Qualities or properties that a product must have:
    • Performance
    • Look and feel
    • Usability
    • Security
    • Legal restriction

Example: The product shall determine friend or foe in less than 0.25 seconds

Example: The product shall be able to be used by travelers in the arrivals hall who do not speak the home language

Constraints:

  • Global issues that shape requirements

Example: The product shall be available at the beginning of the new tax year

What does “Work” represent?

  • More than a computer system(s)
  • A sum total of:
    • Human task
    • Computers
    • Machines/Devices
    • Documents
    • Etc.
  • Description of relevant “business system”

Understanding the context of work

  • What information flow takes place between the work and the outside world?
  • Adjacent systems that make demand or contribute to the world:
    • Automated systems
    • People
    • Departments
    • Organization
  • Adjacent systems:
    • Customers for the service/information produced by work
    • Supply information needed by work
  • How do you identify Adjacent Systems?
    • Interfaces within organization
    • Interfaces outside your organizations
  • Work context should include all parts of existing work – human activities and computer systems

Business Events

  • Business event:
    • Takes place outside the scope of work
    • Signal to work happens through information inflow
    • Work responds to this information flow in some manner
  • Business events initiate a response through business use cases
  • Time triggered business events:
    • Occurs when a pre-arranged time is reached
    • This could be based on:
      • Periodic
      • Time Intervals
      • Time elapsing since a certain business day
  • Response characterized by outflow of information

Business Events and Business Cases

  • Allows for a partitioning of work in a non-subjective way through an identification of external stimuli
  • Helps create cohesive partitioning with minimal interfaces between the pieces (less dependencies):
    • Subsequent independent analysis can be carried out
  • Allows for a direction of investigation of what happens at the time of a business event

More on Business Use Cases

  • Pre-planned response to a Business Event
  • Collection of:
    • Identifiable processes
    • Data (stored and/or retrieved)
    • Outputs
    • Messages
  • Unit of functionality (basis for functional requirements)
  • Business cases can be isolated:
    • Stakeholders with expertise in that part of work can be identified
  • Business Cases are observable
  • Processing for business cases are continuous

Breaking Down Work

  • Characteristics of Business Use Cases that make them suitable for the partitioning:
    • Natural partitions – make obvious and natural contribution to work
    • Minimal Connectivity with other parts of the work
    • Clearly defined scope
    • Rules for defining scope
    • Observable and definable boundaries
    • Recognizable nomenclature
    • Determinable existence
    • One or more expert stakeholders available

Identifying Business Events

  • Information Flows in a context diagram
  • Each information flow is the result of a business event and/or the resultant business use case

Product Use Cases

  • Determined once the correct work of the business use case has been determined
  • It is a part of the business use case, specifically determining the role the product plays in the work’s response
  • In other words – part of the response is handled by the proposed automated system

Looking for Requirements

  • Methodologically running a “net” through the work
  • Two main concerns:
    • Finding all the requirements
    • Finding the correct techniques
  • Experience & Techniques
  • Fundamental truths:
    • Requirements come from people
    • Requirements are not solutions

Key Actor

  • Requirements Analyst:
    • Span both domains – technology and business
    • Translator – but not a passive interpreter
  • Roles of the Requirements Analyst:
    • Observe, learn and understand the work (from the viewpoint of the user)
    • Interpret the work
    • Discover ways of improving work
    • Record results in a manner understandable to the stakeholders

Techniques and their Usefulness

  • Conscious Requirements:
    • Uppermost in the mind
    • Symptomatic of something stakeholders want to improve
  • Unconscious Requirements:
    • Ingrained within stakeholders
    • Often missed because stakeholders assume similar in-depth knowledge of work
  • Undreamed of Requirements:
    • Useful requirements, but not realized as such by the users
    • Missed because of:
      • Lack of technological sophistication
      • Single perspective of work

Where do you start?

  • Business Use Cases:
    • Fundamental to requirements
    • A good starting point
    • Requirements Gathering may be initiated one Business use case at a time

Understanding the current situation

  • Useful in getting a holistic idea of the work
  • Basis from which improvements may be suggested
  • Process Modeling (E.g. DFD of current process, Flow Charts, Activity Diagrams):
    • Working Models – facilitate the asking of the right questions
    • Records the work and documents your understanding
  • Some suggestions:
    • Not attempting to specify a new product
    • Underlying policy (the inner essence) of the business system
    • Attempt to understand the functionalities of the current business system
    • Use it as a guide to make decisions about what needs to be changed and what does not
    • Restrict amount of detail to what is sufficient (the major parts)
    • Cover all the work that can possibly be relevant to the new product:
      • Part of business that could have a positive contribution
      • Areas with operational problems
      • Areas not well understood

Apprenticing

  • Useful for in-house work
  • A means of understanding current work being done
  • Analyst is the apprentice
  • User is the Master craftsman
  • Analyst learns about the work by:
    • Sitting with the user in situ
    • Making observations
    • Asking questions
    • Actually doing some of the work
  • Objective:
    • Observe and abstract away
    • Overcome the users' connection with the physical incarnation of work:
      • Artifacts
      • Technology
  • Use understanding to take advantage of:
    • New technology
    • Streamline processes
    • Simplify work
    • Automate work

Interviewing

  • Fundamental to most elicitation techniques
  • Ideal for eliciting conscious requirements
  • Good idea to send in an agenda before the meeting

Guidelines for Interviews

  • Set the context of the interview
  • Use Business Use Cases as an anchor
  • Question-listen-feedback understanding
  • Draw models to communicate your understanding; elicit feedback
  • Use stakeholder’s terminology and artifact
  • Keep samples/copies of artifacts

Interviews - Asking the right questions

  • How to get unambiguous common understanding
  • Neurolinguistic programming – Meta model of patterns and associated questions:
    • “We get the sales information ….”
    • Unspecified nouns
    • Unspecified verbs
    • Other patterns:
      • Comparison (“better”; Compared to what?)
      • Judgment (Who says it’s better? Who has the authority?)
      • Generalization (“Can’t”, “must”; What prevents you? Why must you?)
      • Universal quantifiers (“never”, “always”; Are there exceptions?)
      • Nominalization – turning verbs into a noun (“Processing renewals over the Internet is better”; What does the author mean by processing? What activities are implied?)

Essence of the work

  • Critical to:
    • Finding the right requirements
    • The right solution (eventually)
  • Stakeholders often speak in terms of solutions
  • The task of the RA is to interpret these and uncover the essence of work
  • Imagine the essence as something existing separately from its implementation

Solving the Right Problem

  • Building a sophisticated technological product is useless unless the users can use it
  • Discovering the right problem to solve is often harder
  • As an Analyst, you need to dig deep and be sure that you are solving the right problem

Scenarios

  • Scenarios are an:
    • Outline of a plot
    • Postulated sequence of steps
  • Scenarios offer a means to:
    • Explore business use cases
    • Discover requirements
  • Business Analysts can use scenarios to get a description of the business use case from an interested stakeholder
  • Description is in natural language so easy for stakeholders to understand
  • Scenario construction may be initiated by asking a stakeholder to describe the work related to a business use case
  • Sketching the scenario:
    • Breaking the description into steps
    • Confirming the steps with the stakeholders
    • Using the initial steps as a base for formalizing the scenario

Formal Scenario Template

  • Business Event name
  • Business Use Case name:
    • Combination of active verb and a direct object
  • Trigger:
    • Data or request for service from an adjacent system
    • Arrival of a temporal condition that activates the use case
  • Preconditions:
    • Conditions that must exist before the use case is valid
    • Sometimes such conditions can be outcomes of another use case
  • Interested Stakeholders:
    • People, organizations and/or computer systems who have knowledge necessary to specify the use case
  • Active stakeholders:
    • People, organizations and/or computer systems that do the work for the use case
  • Normal Case Steps:
    • Steps the use case goes through to complete the normal course of work
    • Specified in natural language statements
  • Alternatives:
    • Acceptable variations on normal case processing
    • Examine each step of normal case and look for instances where steps can be different or the actor can be given a choice
    • Can be helpful in improving work or providing better service

Formal Scenario Template

  • Exceptions:
    • Unwanted but necessary variations
    • Examine each step and ask questions such as:
      • What if step does not complete or cannot be completed or result is unacceptable or wrong?
      • What can go wrong?
      • What could happen to prevent work from reaching the step?
      • Could an external entity disrupt or prevent this step or use case?
      • Could associated technology fail or be unavailable?
      • Could the end user fail to understand what is required? Or misunderstand presented information?
      • Could the end user take a wrong action intentionally/unintentionally or fail to respond?
  • Outcome:
    • Desired situation at the end of the use case

Misuse and Negative Cases

  • Negative or harmful possibilities:
    • Abuse of work
    • Defraud
  • Useful to think in terms of protagonist and antagonist:
    • Protagonist may create unintentional misuse
    • Antagonist intentionally harmful
  • Examine each normal scenario step to see what could go wrong
  • Misuse may result in the stopping of the business use case

What if? Scenarios

  • Can be used to explore possibilities and question business rules
  • May involve questioning a constraint — what if the constraint does not exist?
  • Stimulates creativity and helps produce innovative products

Use Case Diagrams

  • Use case diagrams describe what a system does from the standpoint of an external observer
  • Emphasis is on what a system does rather than how
  • Use case diagrams are closely connected to scenarios
  • Scenario: description of what happens when someone interacts with the system
  • Use case: summary of scenarios for a single task or goal
  • Consists of:
    • Actor — who or what initiates the events involved
    • Communication associate — connection between actor and use case

Use Cases

  • A system boundary rectangle separates the clinic system from the external actors
  • A use case generalization shows that one use case is simply a special kind of another
  • Include relationships factor use cases into additional ones — helpful when the same use case can be factored out of multiple use cases
  • An extend relationship indicates one use case is a variation of another, triggered at specific extension points
  • Use Cases are helpful for:
    • Determining features (requirements)
    • Communicating with clients — simple notation
    • Generating test cases from scenarios

Understanding the Real Problem

  • Software products frequently fail to meet user expectations:
    • Real problem was never stated
    • Never correctly understood
  • Stakeholders often state requirements in terms of an implementation
  • Analysis approaches:
    • Two fundamental objectives:
      • Essence of the work/business requirement
      • Abstraction
  • Thinking above the line

The Essence: What

  • Focus above the line from “how” to “what”
  • During trawling, the stakeholder often describes a solution, not the underlying problem
  • Such solutions are limited by their experience and imagination
  • The analyst needs to interpret and uncover the essence:
    • You will easily uncover the implementation of work
    • Look beyond it to understand the fundamental reason the work exists

The Essence: Why

  • Solve the right problem — often overlooked
  • Avoid re-implementing outmoded technology
  • Understand that both technology and the organization may have changed since the last implementation

The Essence: How

  • Reverse engineer an existing system
  • Ignore technology element
  • Avoid prejudging the implementation

Abstraction

  • The act of obtaining or removing physical implementation
  • Reduce to essential characteristics
  • An idea rather than an implementation

Moving to the Future

  • Requires:
    • Innovative approach by the Analyst
    • Willingness and openness to new ideas by stakeholders
  • Does not mean re-implementation of current work
  • Involves:
    • Being innovative
    • Systemic thinking
    • Providing value
    • Challenging constraints

Being Innovative

  • Not invention, but fresh thinking
  • What individuals spend money on:
    • Pleasure
    • Prestige
    • Convenience
  • From an organizational point of view, most applicable is convenience:
    • Examine user’s point of view
    • Examine user's essential objective: could you reduce the steps?
    • Provide additional services

Systemic Thinking

  • Think of the business as a system
  • Examine aggregation of parts
  • Consider the entirety of the business and how components interact
  • Value demand and failure demand

Value

  • Thinking about value in terms of three factors:
    • Reward
    • Penalty
    • Cost

Challenging Constraints

  • Seriously question constraints and seek to remove them
  • Ask:
    • Is this real?
    • What would happen if the constraint did not exist?
  • Constraints are assumptions made by an organization about how business is done
  • They could influence:
    • Business model
    • Business processes
    • Technology use

Starting the Solution

  • Consider and design the experience resulting from your choice of automation boundary
  • Deliver functionality that:
    • Fits into the users’ work custom
    • Meets operational needs of the organization
    • Contributes to the organization’s goals
    • Does so at a price that pleases the user

Determining the Extent of the Product

  • Task of the business analysis:
    • Determine what work should be done in the future
    • How the product can best contribute to that work
  • As the BUC represent responses to external requests, the ideal response should:
    • Provide the most valuable service
    • At the lowest cost in terms of time, materials, or effort
    • In the most pleasing manner
  • Find the best way to achieve the desired outcome for a BUC

Innovation Trigger

  • Connections:

The Real Origin of the Business Event

  • The origin typically is outside the work
  • The user within the work is part of the response, unless it is a customer-facing system
  • Examine adjacent systems closely

Role of Adjacent Systems

  • Identify real interests/intentions of adjacent systems
  • Determine if the adjacent system needs involvement for a particular work
  • Ask questions about:
    • Technological capabilities
    • Interactive technological capabilities
    • Desired outcome
    • Aspirations
  • Examine the work:
    • Desired outcome
    • What the work wishes to provide or is capable of providing
  • Ignore current technology/organization limitations

Active Adjacent Systems

  • Behave dynamically (usually humans)
  • Can actively participate or interact with work
  • Behavior can be predicted within reason
  • Will respond to signals from work and obey instructions (especially if perceived benefit exists)
  • Response will be suitably quick

Autonomous Adjacent Systems

  • Acts independently from work but has connections to it
  • Typically sends and/or receives one-way data flow
    • Question if this is ideal or a constraint of past decisions
  • Is not involved in business events triggered by it

Co-Operative Adjacent Systems

  • Behave predictably and cooperate during the business use case
  • Typically another automated system

Product Use Cases

  • Determined once the correct work of the business use case is determined
  • Part of the business use case, specifically the role of the product in the response
  • Serve as:
    • Vehicles for discovering and clustering related requirements
    • Planning implementation versions
    • Input to writing test cases
    • Providing business-related basis for simulations and prototypes
    • Making it easier to handle changes, as they trace back to business use cases and events

Functional Requirements

  • Specify what product must do from the perspective of the business
  • Describe actions that product must do complete specific work
  • Represent the functional essence of work
  • Must provide enough detail for the developer to construct the correct product with minimum clarification and explanation from either the analyst or the stakeholders

Finding Functional Requirements

  • Functional requirements may be uncovered from each step of a scenario by asking:
    • What does the product have to do to complete the steps?

Level of Detail or Granularity

  • Typically written as a single statement using a single verb
  • As far as possible requirements should not be compounded
  • A consistent format should be used

Exceptions or Alternatives

  • These are obtained from exception / alternative scenario steps identified for the product use case
  • Procedure remains the same
  • A good practice would be write such requirements to include the exception condition

Avoiding Ambiguity

  • Ambiguities may rise from
    • Idiosyncrasies of the language
    • Lack of clarity about the product context
  • Eliminate pronouns
  • Use naming conventions and definition sections effectively
  • Grouping requirements by use case can help reduce contextual ambiguity

Requirements Not Solution

  • Requirements should reflect the essence of work not its implementation

Grouping Requirements

  • Grouping of requirements enable
    • Discovery of related group of requirements
    • Testing of completeness of the functionality
  • Use Cases provide a logical way to group
  • Alternatives include feature set
  • Essential to create a non-subjective traceable path from one level to another

Non-Functional Requirements

  • Represent the qualities that a product must have
  • Typically enhance the product by making it:
    • Attractive
    • Usable
    • Fast
    • Reliable
    • Etc.

Types of Non-Functional Requirements

  • Look and Feel
  • Usability and humanity
  • Performance
  • Operational
  • Maintainability and Support
  • Security
  • Cultural and Political
  • Legal

Look and Feel

  • Intended spirit, mood, or style of the product’s appearance
  • Simple to use
  • Approachable
  • Authoritative
  • Conforming (to other products or standards)
  • Attractive
  • Innovative

Usability and Humanity

  • Allow a product to conform to:
    • User’s ability
    • User’s expectation of the usage experience
  • Typical Attributes:
    • Easy to use
    • Easy to learn
  • Other areas with regards to usability:
    • Rate of acceptance or adoption
    • Gain of productivity through introduction of product
    • Reduction of error rates
    • Personalization based on user preference
    • Accessibility (this could also be legal)
    • Used by novice users

Performance

  • Important when the product needs to perform tasks with certain amount of speed, accuracy, etc.
  • Related aspects:
    • Speed to complete task
    • Accuracy of results
    • Safety to operator
    • Volumes to be held by product
    • Throughput of transaction
    • Fault tolerance
    • Reliability
    • Scalability of most of the above

Operational Environment

  • Operational requirements describe the environment of the product use
  • Can be ascertained by examining:
    • The operating environment
    • The working condition of the users
    • Partner or collaborating systems

Maintainability and Support

  • May be ascertained by examining expected changes in the:
    • Organization
    • Environment
    • Laws applying to the product
    • Business rules

Security

  • Maybe seen to be concerned with three aspects:
    • Confidentiality
    • Integrity
      • Prevention of unintentional misuse
      • Audit requirements to detect improper usage
      • Proving integrity of product something abnormal happens
    • Availability

Cultural and Political

  • Such requirements may be of the following type:
    • Acceptable solutions
    • Unacceptable solutions
    • Religious observance
    • Political correctness
    • Spelling
    • Etc.

Legal

  • Requirements that require compliance to laws governing:
    • The work environment
    • Obligation to external environment/actors
  • It may also pertain to standards

What do we mean by “fit”?

  • What does fit mean in this context?
    • The solution completely satisfies the requirements
      • It does what it must do
      • Has the property it must have
  • How do we test fit?
    • Requirement or the compliance to it must be measured
    • We measure the requirement by specifying the fit criterion

Why a fit criterion?

  • Provides a mean to demonstrate (through testing) that a product ‘fits’ the requirement
    • By Performing the desired function
    • Possessing the desired property
  • Provides the benchmark to the developers to build the product adhering to a desired standards
  • The objective is to arrive at an agreed upon and appropriate measurement for the requirement/or a means of determining compliance to it

Arriving at a fit criterion

  • Possible criterion
    • Time elapsed before complete adoption
    • User satisfaction level after a period of usage
  • The criterion will be contextual
  • Intention would be find an appropriate scale of measurement
    • Speed of operation – scale is time
    • Usability
      • Time needed to achieve a level of competence
      • Time needed to learn a product
      • Error rate while using it
  • Attempt to discover the rationale or justification for the requirement
    • Facilitates understanding the real need
    • And therefore, the fit criterion
  • Typically a fit criterion may be arrived at by analyzing the description and the rationale for the requirement
  • An alternate approach would be to question stakeholders about what constitutes the failure of a requirement
